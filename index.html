The Six Traps

In a ten-minute consultation, it takes only one misstep to trigger defensiveness or polite disengagement. Recognize how they sound—and how to step out of them.

01 The Trap

The Expert Trap

Stepping in with all the answers

What it sounds like

"What you need to do is cut out carbs after 6pm and start walking 30 minutes every morning."

The Fallout: Reduces the patient to a passive listener who nods politely, but takes zero ownership.

01 The Antidote

Partnership

Two experts in the room

Say this instead

"You know your daily routine better than anyone. What ideas have crossed your mind so far?"

Dr Lindsay's Insight: You bring clinical expertise; they bring life expertise. Invite them to design the strategy.

02 The Trap

The Q&A Trap

Rapid-fire checklist interrogation

What it sounds like

"How many units a week? When did it start? Have you tried patches? Does your partner smoke?"

The Fallout: Trains the patient to give short, defensive one-word answers. It kills authentic flow.

02 The Antidote

Ask & Reflect Ratio

One open question, then reflect

Say this instead

"What's your sense of how smoking fits into your day right now?" — [Listen, then Reflect]

Dr Lindsay's Insight: Aim for at least one reflection for every question you ask. Reflection opens doors; interrogation shuts them.

03 The Trap

Premature Focus

Zooming in on the clinician's agenda

What it sounds like

"Your HbA1c is 68, so we need to tackle your sugar and diet today, before anything else."

The Fallout: If the patient came in terrified about their bereavement or knee pain, you lose their engagement immediately.

03 The Antidote

Agenda Mapping

Agree on the menu first

Say this instead

"There are a few health markers on my screen, but first: what feels most important for you to address today?"

Dr Lindsay's Insight: Lay out the agenda together. When patients choose the starting point, their buy-in triples.

04 The Trap

The Labelling Trap

Slapping diagnostic categories

What it sounds like

"You have a real issue with non-compliance when it comes to taking your blood pressure tablets."

The Fallout: Labels spark identity debates. The consultation becomes an argument about the word, not the behavior.

Tap to see Antidote
04 The Antidote

Focus on Experience

Describe what happens, without the label

Say this instead

"Let's look at what typically gets in the way on the days you don't manage to take the medication."

Dr Lindsay's Insight: You don't need a patient to accept a label to help them change a habit. Side-step the label entirely.

05 The Trap

The Blaming Trap

Searching for fault and causality

What it sounds like

"How did you let your weight creep up by another two stone since October?"

The Fallout: Triggers intense shame or anger. When patients feel accused, they retreat into sustain talk to defend themselves.

05 The Antidote

No-Fault Curiosity

Replace judgment with exploration

Say this instead

"Life has been hectic since the autumn. What's made the last few months particularly tough to manage?"

Dr Lindsay's Insight: Blame looks backward at failure; curiosity looks forward at possibility.

06 The Trap

The Chat Trap

Comfortable, surface-level small talk

What it sounds like

"How are the grandkids? Lovely weather this week! Have you been keeping busy in the garden?"

The Fallout: Pleasant and warm, but consumes 8 minutes of a 10-minute appointment without touching the reason they came.

06 The Antidote

Purposeful Transition

Warmly pivot to the clinical heart

Say this instead

"It's great to hear the family is well. In our remaining time, I want to make sure we give full focus to your breathlessness."

Dr Lindsay's Insight: Empathy does not mean endless drifting. Kindness has a clock in general practice.

Clinician Self-Care & Capacity

The Control Compass

Clinician burnout happens when we take responsibility for decisions that belong entirely to the patient. Motivational interviewing frees you from carrying what isn't yours.

What I Cannot Control

Release responsibility here

  • • Whether the patient takes their medication once they leave the surgery.
  • • Their social circumstances, housing stress, or family pressures.
  • • Whether they are ready to quit smoking today.
  • • Their ambivalence—it belongs to them, not you.

What I Can Control

Invest your energy here

  • • My own tone, body language, and avoidance of the righting reflex.
  • • Asking permission before giving medical advice.
  • • Reflecting change talk when it emerges, instead of letting it slip by.
  • • Creating a consulting space where the patient feels respected and unjudged.
